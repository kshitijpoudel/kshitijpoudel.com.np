What is Real Picture Assessment?

It's how the bank figures out the true financial health of a small business — beyond just the official tax documents. The Loan Officer (LO) visits the business in person and builds a financial picture from scratch.

🎯 The big goal: Determine if the borrower can repay (ability) and will repay (willingness).

The 3 stages of credit analysis

1

Preparation — Screen the client, fill the loan application, agree on site visit time

2

Site Visit — Collect and verify information at the business and household

3

Analysis — Process the data: build Assets/Liabilities table, Income/Expenses table, calculate ratios, cross-check

The golden rule: Conservative approach

Assets & Income

Record at minimum fair value. When in doubt, take the lower number.

Liabilities & Expenses

Record at maximum available amount. Include everything.

The Site Visit

The LO visits the actual business location to see things with their own eyes — not just trust documents.

Who must be present?

  • The business owner or key shareholder (knows the full picture)
  • The accountant or person handling records
  • Managers of specific activities, if multiple businesses

What to check at the business

CheckWhat to do
Business locationVerify address matches application. Look for signs, registration details, actual open hours.
Competitive positionHow visible is it? How many nearby competitors? What are their prices?
Premises conditionIs it maintained? Is equipment working? Is stock adequate?
DocumentsCheck ownership/lease agreements, licenses, contracts with suppliers and buyers
Inventory / stockPhysically count the goods. Don't just accept a list.
RecordsReview notebooks, Excel files, accounting software — whatever they use

💡 Key technique: Ask the same questions multiple times in different ways. Consistent answers = reliable information.

Household visit (optional, but mandatory for first-time micro borrowers)

Collect info on family expenses, extra income sources, and major one-time spending (like house renovation, weddings).

Socio-economic profile — willingness to repay

Even if the numbers look good, the borrower may not want to repay. Look at:

  • Reputation in the community (ask neighbors, suppliers)
  • Past payment discipline with banks and informal lenders
  • Personal factors: age, health, education, family situation, attitude to alcohol/gambling
  • Does profit get reinvested in business or spent personally?

Assets & Liabilities Table

Think of it like a business balance sheet — but built by the LO from what they saw during the visit.

Assets = Liabilities + Equity (Net Worth)
Equity = Total Assets − Total Liabilities

Fixed Assets (long-term, used in business)

  • Equipment, machinery — at current fair market value (can discount 10–20%)
  • Vehicles — check registration certificate
  • Real estate and land — ownership certificate required

Liabilities

TypeExamplesNote
Short-term (<1 year)Accounts payable, short-term loans, unpaid rent/tax/salariesIf goods are in stock on credit, the payable must also appear in liabilities

⚠️ Unused loan limits are NOT liabilities. Only include what has actually been borrowed and not yet repaid.

What happens when a transaction occurs?

EventEffect on table
Receive a loan (NPR 1,000,000)Cash ↑ 1M, Long-term loans ↑ 1M. Equity unchanged.
Buy goods for cash (1,000,000)Cash ↓ 1M, Inventory ↑ 1M. Nothing else changes.
Repay a loan (100,000)Cash ↓ 100K, Short-term loans ↓ 100K. Equity unchanged.
Sell goods for 360K (20% markup)Cash ↑ 360K, Inventory ↓ 300K (cost), Equity ↑ 60K (profit)

💡 Selling goods is the ONLY transaction that changes equity — because the gross profit (markup) is new value created.

Income & Expenses

This covers the last 12 full calendar months before the site visit (not the fiscal year). It tells you whether the business is profitable enough to repay a loan.

How revenue is calculated (accrual basis)

Revenue is counted when goods are delivered — not when cash is received. So if goods were shipped in March but paid in April, it counts as March revenue.

How to find Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

You rarely get a direct cost figure. Instead, use the markup:

Cost of Goods Sold = Revenue ÷ (1 + Markup %)

Example

Revenue = NPR 1,000,000 | Markup = 20%

COGS = 1,000,000 ÷ 1.20 = NPR 833,333

Gross Profit = 1,000,000 − 833,333 = NPR 166,667

Markup formula (important!)

Markup % = (Gross Profit ÷ COGS) × 100
Selling Price = Cost × (1 + Markup %)

Example: Cost = 100, Markup = 20% → Selling Price = 120

Net profit (available for loan repayment)

Net Profit = Operating Profit + Other income − Household expenses − Personal loan installments

💡 Personal loan installments use full amount (principal + interest), unlike business loans which only include interest in expenses.

Financial Ratios

These ratios tell you if the business is creditworthy. Memorize the formula and the passing threshold for each.

Debt-to-Equity (D/E)
D/E = (STL + LTL) ÷ Equity
✓ Good: less than 4.0

Measures how much of the business is funded by debt vs. the owner's own money. Lower = safer.

Liquidity Ratio (Current Ratio)
LR = Short-term Assets ÷ Short-term Liabilities
✓ Good: 1.25 or more

Can the business pay off its short-term debts using current assets? Higher = more liquid.

Net Working Capital / Loan
NWC/LA = (Short-term Assets − STL) ÷ Loan Amount
✓ Good: 1.33 or more

Only for working capital loans. Is there enough working capital to justify the loan size?

Debt Service Coverage (DSCR)
DSCR = Net Profit ÷ Total Annual Loan Payments
✓ Good: more than 1.25

For term loans. Does the profit cover the repayments comfortably?

Interest Coverage (ICR)
ICR = Net Profit ÷ Total Annual Interest
✓ Good: more than 1.5

For overdrafts and revolving credit lines. Can profits cover at least the interest?

Quick reference

Example: DSCR calculation

Net Profit = NPR 500,000/year | Total loan installments next year = NPR 360,000

DSCR = 500,000 ÷ 360,000 = 1.39 Pass ✓

If DSCR were 1.10, it would fail (below 1.25).

Cross-checking

Cross-checking verifies whether the client's numbers are consistent and trustworthy — especially for information that can't be confirmed by documents.

Method 1: Equity cross-check (most important)

The idea: what the owner has today should equal what they started with + what they've earned − what they've spent/withdrawn.

Expected Equity = Starting Capital + Accrued Profit + Investments − Withdrawals
Accrued Profit = Annual Net Profit × Years in business

✅ If actual equity and expected equity differ by less than 20%, the data is considered reliable.

Example

Business started 10 years ago with NPR 500,000. Annual profit = NPR 200,000.

Expected equity ≈ 500,000 + (200,000 × 10) = NPR 2,500,000

If actual equity (from Assets − Liabilities) = 2,300,000 → difference is 8% → Acceptable

If actual equity = 1,500,000 → difference is 40% → Investigate!

What causes the gap? (Scenarios)

Actual < ExpectedActual > Expected
Hidden liabilitiesUndisclosed liabilities inflating equity
Withdrawals not disclosedAssets not counted in previous visit
Assets overvaluedNew investment by owner
Profit overstatedProfit understated

Method 2: Cashbox–Sales check

Cash at day-end + Cash spent during day = Annual sales ÷ 365

Rough check: does the cash in the till match the average daily sales?

Method 3: Inventory flow check

Inventory (today) = Inventory (last visit) + Purchases − Cost of Goods Sold

💡 Use purchase price (not selling price) throughout this check.

Method 4: Receivables check

Receivables (today) = Receivables (last visit) + Sales − Cash received

Example

Receivables last visit = 160,000 | Sales this period = 3,800,000 | Cash collected = 3,650,000

Expected receivables = 160,000 + 3,800,000 − 3,650,000 = NPR 310,000

If within ±10% of actual → Reliable
